the beginning feels slow? Or maybe the
middle just sags and you find yourself
writing scenes that don't really go
anywhere? It's one of the most common
problems writers face and it's
incredibly frustrating. If you're new to
this channel, my name is Jason Hamilton.
I'm someone who's been spending years of
my life studying story craft. I've
learned that this is almost an always a
structural problem, not an idea problem.
And that's why today we're looking at a
framework designed specifically to solve
this. James Scott Bell's a disturbance
and two doorways. This structure is all
about creating immediate threatening
stakes and then using two major decision
points to propel your hero straight into
the final conflict with no time for
sagging middles. I'm on a quest to cover
all of the major story structures for
novelists. And if you have one that you
want to see explained, just tell me
about it in the comments. At its heart,
Belle's structure is deceptively simple.
It has three core components: a
disturbance, a first doorway, and a
second doorway. Think of them as three
explosions that propel your character
through the story. But understanding
what makes each one work is the key to
unlocking its power. So, let's start
with pillar number one, the disturbance.
The disturbance is not just an inciting
incident. It's the moment the
protagonist's world is ruptured by a
genuine credible threat. Belle's
philosophy is that every great story is
about a character fighting some form of
death. Now, he doesn't just mean
physical death. He outlines three types.
We have, of course, physical death, the
classic life or death scenario. We have
professional death or the threat of
losing a career, a calling or one's
place in society. We have psychological
death, the danger of losing one's
identity, sanity, or the core of who
they are. An effective disturbance must
threaten your protagonist in one of
these three ways. If your character is
merely curious about the event, the
story has no engine. A strange letter
arrives and they wonder what it means.
That's not a disturbance. The character
can just throw the letter away and go
back to watching TV. A weak disturbance
lets the character off the hook. A real
disturbance hooks them because the
consequences of ignoring it are
catastrophic. For instance, in The
Umbrella Academy, the death of the
siblings adopted father is the initial
disturbance. It forces them back to
their childhood home. But the real
threat is what they discover there, a
looming apocalypse. That's not something
that you can just ignore. It creates an
immediate sense of urgency that demands
a response. This is a little bit more
than a gentle nudge. It's a violent
shove into the story. The disturbance
must make inaction impossible, which
leads the character directly to the
first major choice. So, we move now to
pillar number two, the first doorway.
After the disturbance shatters the
protagonist world, they arrive at the
first doorway. This is the point of no
return. Once the character walks through
this door, their old life is gone
forever. And this is where they commit,
willingly or not, to engaging with the
central conflict. This doorway
corresponds to the break between act one
and act two in the traditional three-act
structure, but Belle is specific about
its function and timing. He recommends
it happen before the 20% mark of their
novel. Why so early? Well, because novel
readers need to feel the main action
starting quickly to stay invested for
hundreds of pages. The first doorway is
that moment that the story truly begins.
The key here is commitment. The
character can't just dip a toe into the
water. They have to dive in. In the
young adult fantasy, Children of Blood
and Bone, the protagonist, Isle, has to
decide whether to trust the gods and try
to bring magic back, knowing it will
make her a target. Remaining passive
guarantees the death of her people's
identity, and only by acting, by going
through the doorway, does she have a
chance at all. The commitment doesn't
have to be a heroic one. A character
could be blackmailed into action or make
a desperate choice out of fear. It
doesn't matter why they go through the
door, only that they do, and that the
door locks behind them. There's no going
back to the way things were. This
irrevocable choice is what gives the
story its forward momentum and keeps the
middle from sagging. But that long
middle section needs more than just
momentum. It needs a turning point. That
brings us to pillar number three, the
second doorway. The journey between the
two doorways is the treacherous
territory of act two, where your hero
faces escalating trials. But it all
builds towards the second doorway. If
the first door was a commitment to the
fight, the second is a commitment to the
final battle. But there are no more
chances for negotiation, compromise, or
escape. Belle describes this doorway as
the one that usually leads to a
disaster. It's often triggered by a
massive crisis or setback. The
protagonist plans have failed. Their
allies may have abandoned them, and
everything looks hopeless. This is the
classic all is lost moment. The hero is
stripped of their illusions and forced
to face the final antagonist, often when
they feel least prepared. Think of it
this way. The first doorway is the
character saying, "Okay, I'll fight."
The second doorway is the moment they
realize the fight is here now, and it's
to the death. Psychologically, this
doorway collapses all remaining distance
between the protagonist and the climax.
It makes the final confrontation feel
not just exciting, but inevitable. All
other options have been systematically
stripped away, leaving only one path
forward, straight through the fire. So,
we have a disturbance and two doorways.
Great. But that still leaves about half

How do you stop it from turning into a
long, boring slog between two exciting
points? Belle provides another crucial

at the 50% mark, the structural midpoint
of the novel. It's when your protagonist
is forced to stop and take a hard look
at themselves. They have to confront the
question, who have I become or what must
I do to win? It's the story
psychological fulcrum where the
character's internal journey pivots. In
a plot- driven story like say the

mirror moment forces a change in
