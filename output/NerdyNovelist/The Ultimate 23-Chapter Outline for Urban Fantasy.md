Urban fantasy is deceptive. On the
surface, it looks like a story about
magic, but structurally, it's actually a
hard-boiled detective novel wearing a
wizard hat. If you miss the specific
beats of a procedural mystery, the whole
story falls apart. Hi guys, my name is
Jason Hamilton. I'm a fantasy author and
I used to work for Kindle Prneur, where
I spent years studying how books are
written and sold. But really, I'm just
the most obsessed storytelling nerd that
I know. And to figure out how urban
fantasy, this specific standard urban
fantasy, actually works, I didn't just
guess. I did a deep dive on actual
bestsellers in this genre, books with
over a thousand reviews and a 4.5 star
average or higher. I used a mixture of
my own analysis and AI to pick them
apart scene by scene. And what I found
was shockingly consistent. In this case,
I've formed it into a 23 chapter
template that practically guarantees a
page turner. So, let's break down that
template. Starting with act one, which
includes the setup and the squeeze, we
have to start by looking at the
foundation. You can't just drop a wizard
into a city and hope for the best. You
need constraint. You need pressure.
Chapter 1 is distinct in urban fantasy
because it functions as the double life.
We aren't just seeing the protagonist
existing. We are seeing them juggle.
They are usually navigating a gritty,
mundane environment. think paying rent,
fixing a car, or working a dead-end job
while managing a hidden dangerous aspect
of their identity. Maybe it's a
concealed magical heritage, or maybe
they are managing an addiction to a
curse. This chapter needs to accomplish
two things. First, it gives us a save
the cat moment where they handle a minor
conflict that demonstrates they are
hyper competent but desperately trying
to stay under the radar. Second, it
highlights their fear. The status quo is
a house of cards. They are barely
holding it together. And on the
periphery, we see the first sign that
this anonymity is about to end. Then we
shatter that illusion in chapter 2, the
intrusion. That threat we glimpsed, it
kicks down the front door. And this is
your inciting incident. A magical
creature rampages through a non-magical
zone or a corpse shows up with arcane
runes carved into it. And this event
strips away the protagonist safety net.
They lose their job, their apartment, or
their disguise. Things get upended.
Crucially, this is where you also
introduce the rival or the love
interest. In this genre, they are often
the same person. This character
represents the elite magical society
that the protagonist hates or is going
to come to hate. They are powerful,
arrogant, and create immediate friction.
This sizzle is vital because it
establishes the class difference between
your street level hero and the ivory
tower magical establishment. Chapter
three is the squeeze. The protagonist
tries to do damage control. They want to
go back to being a nobody, but the
narrative won't let them. The mundane
authorities or magical gatekeepers back
them into a corner with an ultimatum. Go
to magical prison or accept a suicide
mission from the arrogant elite faction
that we just met. They try to refuse,
but the stakes are personal. Maybe they
have a little sister who is threatened
or need some money for a specific
desperate reason. Whatever the case is,
they have no choice. That leads us
directly to chapter 4, crossing the
threshold, and they sign the deal. They
step out of the m mundane world and
enter into the special world like a
hidden guild hall or a supernatural
bureau. They are a fish out of water
here, looked down upon by the snobs in
robes. This is also where you usually
introduce the sidekick or the mentor,
someone who isn't trying to kill them or
arrest them, giving the reader a little
breathing room before the real work
begins. That leads us to act two, part
one, which is the investigation. So, now
we are in the thick of it. The hero is
in the world. do they have the mission
and they have a handler they can't
stand. Chapter 5 is the uneasy alliance.
The protagonist is forced to sit down
with that rival or love interest to
review the case. We need to see a
transfer of respect here. Through a
briefing or a sparring session, the
elite rival realizes the street rat
knows things that they don't. We call
this the competence. The romantic or
sexual tension spikes not because they
are flirting, but because they are
arguing about methods, and it sets up
the enemies to partners dynamic that
empowers the be story. In chapter 6, the
investigation actually starts. They hit
the streets. They go to a CD magical
nightclub or a black market alchemy
shop, places that the elite rival
wouldn't be caught dead in. The
protagonist uses their street smarts to
get a clue. But here's the twist. The
simple case of the murdered goblin or
the stolen wand, whatever it is, isn't
that simple. It connects to a darker
ancient conspiracy involving forbidden
magic like necromancy or blood magic or
something of that nature. So, the scope
just widened. Following that lead brings
us to chapter 7, the test. This is your
first major action setpiece, often
called the first pinch point. The team
walks into an ambush. A secondary
monster or enforcer tries to wipe them
out, and during the chaos, the
protagonist is separated from their
powerful handler. They have to survive
alone. This scares them not just because
they might die, but because they almost
reveal their secret power to survive.
They managed to hide it, but it was
close. Chapter 8, The Comedown, provides
a necessary breather. After the
adrenaline of the ambush, the
protagonist and the love interest share
a quiet moment. Maybe they're just
patching up wounds or sharing a drink in
a safe house. And the barriers drop. We
learn that the perfect arrogant elite
has their own scars. The protagonist
starts to feel guilty for lying to
someone who just was watching their
back. And this deepens the bond and
raises the emotional stakes. Chapter
nine is the red herring. Based on
everything that they found, the team
identifies a suspect like a rival gang
leader or a shady warlock. And they plan
a raid. The protagonist has to take a
big risk, maybe breaking a law or using
a sketchy artifact to make the bust
happen. They execute the plan, the doors
get kicked in, and they think they've
won. But readers of the genre know
better because we haven't even hit the
middle of the book yet. Chapter 10 is
the midpoint disaster. The raid reveals
that the suspect is innocent or just a
pawn. The true villain is someone much
scarier, but the real kick in the teeth
is personal. While the protagonist was
busy playing detective, the villain
struck at their home. A dependent is
kidnapped or a mentor is attacked or
their sanctuary is burned to the ground.
The stakes shift instantly from I'm
doing this job to stay out of jail to I
am going to kill whoever did this. The
protagonist stops being reactive and
becomes active. They are no longer a
victim of circumstances. They are now a
hunter. By the way, if you'd like to

to act two, part two. After the
disaster, we have to pick up the pieces.
So, chapter 11 is assessing the damage.
The protagonist is reeling from guilt.
The magical authorities usually try to
pull them off the case, claiming they
are too emotional, and this is a key
beat. The love interest intervenes. They
defy their own bosses to stick with the
protagonist, and this cementss the
alliance. They go rogue, setting up a
secret location to finish this off the
books. Chapter 12 is where we get the
exposition dump that doesn't feel like a
dump. They look at ancient texts or
visit an oracle or figure out what the
villain is really doing. They discover
some kind of mcguffin that is linked to
the protagonist's specific legacy or
bloodline. And this isn't a coincidence.
They were drawn into this because they
are the only ones who can stop it. And
destiny has entered the chat. Now we
need an tonal shift. Chapter 13 is the
heist prep. To stop the villain, they
need to get into some high security
location, a gala or a vampire ball or a
secure vault. And we get the fun of
planning, logistics, and the comedic
friction of the rough protagonist having
to learn etiquette or wear a tuxedo.
It's the sort of calm before the storm.
The love interest helps them armor up,
and we get a moment charged with
admiration and desire. Chapter 14 is the
masquerade. The infiltration happens.
They aim to blend in. The tension comes
from maintaining the cover while hunting
for the objective. And we usually get a
pink romantic moment here, like a dance
or stolen whisper in a corner before
reality crashes back in. They spot the
villain, but it feels wrong. It's too
easy. Now, chapter 15 is the trap
springs. The villain knew they were
coming. Chaos flows. The protagonist is
cornered, and the villain offers a
choice. Join them and surrender the
power or watch the team die. The
protagonist refuses, naturally, but the
villain unleashes some kind of
devastating attack. This is your second
pinch point. It shows just how
outclassed our heroes are. Chapter 16 is
the retreat. They barely get out alive.
They lose the mcguffin or the rescue
fails. They are battered, bleeding, and
morale is at a zero. The protagonist
blames themsself. And to make matters
worse, the love interest usually takes a
hit meant for the hero. They retreat to
a place of last resort, effectively
admitting defeat, and this is the all is
lost moment. Chapter 17 is the dark
knight. Isolated, the protagonist
confronts their internal demons. They
think about running away, but a
conversation, maybe with the sidekick or
the injured love interest,
recontextualizes the struggle. They
realize that the curse or monster side
of them that they've been hiding is
actually a weapon. They decide to stop
suppressing it, and they accept their
identity. And that leads us directly
into act three. This final act is a
sprint. Chapter 18 is the rally. The
protagonist gathers what's left of the
team. and they come up with a desperate,
high-risk plan that relies entirely on
the protagonist's unique, formally
hidden abilities, and this is where the
found family aspect shines. Everyone
steps up. The protagonist arms
themselves, symbolizing that they are no
longer hiding. Chapter 19 is storming
the stronghold. The final battle kicks
off. The side characters peel off to
handle minions or disable wards,
channeling the action, so the
protagonist is funneled toward the boss.
The pacing here should be frantic and
brutal. Chapter 20, the confrontation
pits the hero against the villain. The
villain is on the verge of victory,
completing the ritual. The hero uses
some kind of conventional attacks like
guns or standard spells, but it does
nothing. They are pushed to the brink of
death, and it looks impossible. Then we
get chapter 21, the unleashing. This is
why readers buy these books. With no
other options, the protagonist
voluntarily breaks the seal. They
unleash their legacy or forbidden magic
in some kind of spectacular, terrifying
display. It's the Super Saiyan moment.
This power overwhelms the villain and
destroys the threat, but it leaves the
protagonist drained and exposed, and the
secret is out. Everyone sees what they
really are. Chapter 22 is when the dust
settles. In the immediate aftermath, the
authorities arrive. The protagonist
expects to be arrested for using some
kind of forbidden magic, but the partner
or a high-ranking mentor intervenes.
They get a pardon or a cover up based on
their heroism, and victims are reunited.
The adrenaline crashes into exhaustion.
Finally, chapter 23 in the new reality.
We fast forward a few days and the
protagonist has healed, but the status
quo from chapter 1 is gone forever. They
can't go back to the shadows. They
accept a permanent role in the magical
world or set up shop as a private
investigator. The relationship with the
love interest is solidified into a
partner dynamic, and the protagonist
looks out at the city ready for the next
monster. Now, you might be thinking,
that's a great structure for book one,
but what about book two or book 10? as
long series are very common in urban
fantasy. The common writing advice is
that it depends. I of course hate that
answer. And while sequels have
variables, the DNA of a bestseller
remains more or less consistent. You
can't just copy and paste the template,
though, because the first book is an
origin story. The hero has already
crossed the threshold. So, here's what
changes for the sequels. First, the
double life chapter transforms into the
new world. You don't need to prove they
are mundane anymore. You show them
running their agency or working for the
bureau and the refusal of the call
disappears or becomes very weak. In a
sequel, the hero is more proactive. They
are a problem solver and we don't need
three chapters of them debating if magic
is real. Second, the stakes must
escalate. In book one, the threat is
more local, maybe a killer in the city.
But by book two, it's political or
regional. And by book four, it's
existential, the end of the world, that
kind of thing. You cannot repeat the
same small-cale murder mystery 10 times
without weaving in a larger threat. But
here's what stays the same, and this is
where most authors mess up. You must
keep the case of the month. Urban
fantasy is a procedural. Even if there's
a looming war with the faye, every book
needs a kind of specific contained plot
that is resolved by the end. You need an
inciting incident, an investigation, and
a villain that gets taken down. If you
write a book that is just characters
talking about their feelings without a
mystery, your sales will tank. You also
keep the midpoint disaster. You keep the
friction with authority, and you
absolutely keep the power level reveal
in the climax. It becomes about mastery
rather than discovery. Readers crave
that moment when the hero does something
with their magic that leaves everyone
staring in shock. So don't remove that.
This template is not a cage. It's a set
of building blocks. Use the pinch

respect the genre expectations. If you
