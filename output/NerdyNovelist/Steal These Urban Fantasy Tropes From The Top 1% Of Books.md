You can create a brilliant magic system,
but if you scrap the character dynamics,
your urban fantasy novel is going to
flop. Readers in this genre have a very
specific expectation from how the
reluctant hero refuses to call to how
the slow burn romance actually burns.
Merry Christmas everybody. My name is
Jason. I'm a fantasy author and a
massive nerd for narrative structure.
And this week, we're doing something a
little different. This is going to be
the first of three videos going into
depth on a specific genre. in this case,
urban fantasy. If all goes well, I might
be able to make a lot more videos like
this on very specific genres throughout
all of Amazon. So, let me know in the
comments if you have a particular genre
you'd like me to cover. Now, I didn't
want to just guess what works. So, I
conducted a massive analysis of the top
performing books in urban fantasy,
specifically standard urban fantasy,
because there are a number of
variations. and I used a mixture of AI
and my own manual review to break down
the bestsellers that have proven
themselves in the market. And today, I'm
sharing the specific tropes that
separate the one-star reviews from the
five-star favorites. First is the
masquerade. If you're writing urban
fantasy, you need to start with the
masquerade. And this isn't optional.
This is a defining line that separates
this genre from a story that just
happens to include wizards. It creates
the tension between the boring, mundane
world your reader lives in and the
thrilling supernatural world that you
are inventing. Readers want to look out
their window at a dark alley and wonder
if a vampire is stalking prey there
unseen by human eyes. But if everyone in
your book knows about magic, you lose
that tension immediately and you lose
the mystery. So you have to decide
exactly how the secret is kept. Is it
fear-based? Maybe the supernatural
beings hide because they are terrified
of what humans would do if they found
out. Or maybe it's passive. Magic might
just naturally hide itself, scrambling
the brains of anyone who isn't
initiated. You could also go the men in
black route and have a specific
organization dedicated to wiping
memories and destroying evidence.
Whatever you pick, you have to address
the elephant in the room, technology. It
was easy to hide a werewolf in the
1980s, but today every person on the
street has a 4K camera in their pocket
connected to the internet. Does magic
scramble digital sensors? Does the
government scrub YouTube videos of
spellcasting? You have to answer this
and if you don't the reader is going to
check out because their suspension of
disbelief will snap. So pick a system,
stick to the rules and make the reader
believe that this could be real. The
next trope is the reluctant hero. So
once you have the world, you need
someone to live in it. And that's where
we get the reluctant hero. This trope
works because it provides the most
honest reaction to finding out monsters
are real. If you discovered a werewolf
was eating people in your neighborhood,
you wouldn't grab a kitchen knife and
run outside. you would probably just
lock your doors and look for an
apartment in a different city. And this
is why this protagonist works. They are
the avatar for the reader. They don't
choose heroism. They are kind of forced
into it. But where I see authors get
this wrong is making the reluctance
superficial. They make the character
just kind of shy. And that isn't enough.
The reluctance needs to come from a
valid internal struggle. Maybe they want
to protect their family and getting
involved puts them at risk. Maybe they
just need to pay rent and fighting
demons doesn't pay the bills. Now, let
the practical concerns of real life
compete with the magical crisis. Have
them try to delegate the responsibility.
Have them pack a bag to leave town. Then
hit them with the catalyst, the moment
where inaction becomes impossible. And
even then, let them fight while
resenting the fight. When a character
who desperately wants safety chooses
danger, that's when you have a story.
The next trope is hidden heritage.
Often, that hero discovers they aren't
just an overworked barista, they have
something more. This is the hidden
heritage trope. It validates the secret
hope that we all have that we are
special. But be careful. It is
incredibly easy to make this feel cheap.
You can't just have your character wake
up with laser eyes on a Tuesday for no
reason. You need to know the mechanism
of discovery. Is it biological? Did they
touch a cursed object or was it a
traumatic trigger? And you need to know
the logic behind the secret. Why was it
hidden? Was their grandmother holding
back their powers to protect them? Was
their memory wiped? The most important
part isn't the power itself. It's more
the cost. Gaining power usually means
losing normaly. And once your
protagonist unlocks this part of
themselves, they are forced to lie to
their friends. They have to navigate the
politics of a magical society they
didn't even know existed 5 minutes ago.
So don't treat it as a powerup, treat it
as a burden. This leads to the
grandfather of all tropes, the chosen
one. And in high fantasy, being chosen
is often an honor. In the modern world,
it's a massive inconvenience. Your
protagonist just wants to live a normal
life and a prophecy ruins that. The
conflict shouldn't be about fulfilling
the prophecy. It should be about
resisting it. But do not use the
prophecy as plot armor that guarantines
a win. The stakes evaporate if the
reader knows the hero is destined to
succeed. And instead, make the destiny a
target on their back. Make it cost them
something. Their relationships, their
safety, or a piece of their soul. Modern
audience love agency. Rather than an
immutable fate that forces the character
down a track, make the destiny a choice.
The prophecy opens the doors, but the
character has to walk through it. When
they accept the responsibility, despite
the heavy cost, you have a hero worth
rooting for. To get that hero moving,
you need a mcguffin. And this is the
engine that drives the car. It doesn't
really matter if it's a ring, a sword,
or a flash drive. It only matters that
everybody wants it desperately. In urban
fantasy, this acts as a kind of magnet.
It forces the vampires, the wizards, and
the police into the same room. Don't
just stick a magic object in the story
and give them something to do. Connect
it to emotional stakes. If the hero is
looking for a sword, for example, it
should be the only thing that can
protect their sister. Uh, link the
object to the character's internal
desires and give it history. Urban
fantasy readers love lore, so
referencing ancient treaties or dead
sorcerers makes the world feel real.
Next, we have magic with costs. If your
protagonist can wave a wand and solve
every problem without breaking a sweat,
you don't really have a story. You have
a cheat code. Readers need to know that
using magic is a calculated risk. So,
establish a specific economy. Is there a
physical toll? Does casting a spell
cause bleeding or exhaustion? Is there a
mental strain that erodess their sanity?
Or does it require resources like rare
components? Whatever you pick, stay
consistent. If the tank is empty in
chapter one, don't let them just throw
fireballs like confetti in the climax
just because it looks cool. Real costs
force the character to make a moral
choice. Is casting the spell worth the
physical damage? Is saving the city
worth losing a memory? When you force
your characters to weigh the magical
solution against a heavy cost, you
create genuine tension. Now, we get the
trope called fish out of water. And this
typically goes handinhand with the
masquerade and the magic system. The
fish out of water trope places a
character in a environment where their
nature clashes with their surroundings.
You can have the human thrust into the
magical underground or the ancient
supernatural being trying to navigate
the modern world. So, think of a fay
trying to use an Uber or a vampire
confused by Instagram. It's funny, but
don't stop at the comedy. The true power
here is disorientation. A human
misunderstanding magical etiquette
shouldn't just be awkward. It should be
dangerous. It could start a war. The
best use of this trope is to flip the
script. Transform the character's
displacement into their greatest asset.
Because they are an outsider, they see
solutions the locals overlook. A human
might ask a logical question about a
blood feud that no one else dared to
ask. And you don't have to have them
fully assimilate by the end. Keep that
friction. It makes them distinct. Next
is found family. And if there's one
trope that makes readers in the genre
feel warm and fuzzy, it's the found
family. These characters are usually
outcasts, refugees from supernatural
hierarchies or humans who know too much.
They don't fit into the mundane world or
the magical ones so they kind of just
find each other. The big mistake I see
is introducing everyone at once. So
don't dump five new characters on your
reader in chapter 3. Build a team
organically. Introduce them through
necessity. And maybe your protagonist
needs a hacker, then later needs a
healer. The why matters here. It
shouldn't be because they all just like
each other. The best crews form out of
survival. They are forced together by a
massive threat and this creates
friction. When you have a vampire who
belongs to a coven and a werewolf who
answers to the pack, you force them to
choose between their biological family
and their chosen family. And that choice
is a gold mine for conflict. Use the
shared trauma of saving the world to
glue them together. Next, we get some
buddy cop dynamics. This often leads you
take two people who have no business
working together and handcuff them to
the same case. This works because
contrast creates conflict. In urban
fantasy, this is about worldview. One
represents the streets and the law. The
other represents magic and chaos. Don't
make the human useless. They should be
hypercompetent in their own lane. And
the wizard kills the demon, the cop gets
the warrant, etc. Keep the thorns.
Resist the urge to have them hold hands
by chapter 5. Bind them legally or
magically so they can't walk away. And
then let them argue over methods. The
friction reveals great character depth.
The next one is enemies to lovers. If
you want to take that friction and add
romance, you get the genre favorite
enemies to allies to lovers. This
mirrors the high stakes of the world.
Shallow dating doesn't cut it when the
apocalypse is coming. You need a
connection forged in fire. But please do
not make the enemy abusive. The
animosity should be ideological, not
toxic. Maybe they belong to waring
factions or have different views on
justice. They should respect each
other's skills even if they hate their
goals. Structure the progression.
hatred, grudging recognition, forced
alliance, and finally romantic
awareness. And remember, slow burn does
not mean slow pacing. Do not stop the
plot for googly eyes. Generally, the
romance should be a subplot running
parallel to the action because remember,
this is standard urban fantasy, not
paranormal romance, which is a separate
genre. Layer small moments of intimacy
throughout the danger so that the final
kiss feels inevitable. The next trope is
the ticking clock. To keep that hero
moving against the villain, you need a
ticking clock. If your plot is dragging,
you are probably missing a deadline.
Urgency sharpens decision-making. When a
character has a month, they can be
rational. When they have until sunset
before the virus becomes permanent, they
have to take risks. So, don't make the
deadline arbitrary. Ground it in the
mechanics of the world. Maybe a ritual
requires specific star alignment or a
poison moves at a certain speed. And
keep the clock visible. Show the moon
rising. Show the corruption spreading.
This is great for interworld friction.
If the hero has to stop a demon by 5:00
pm, but has a job interview at 4:30, you
have immediate relatable conflict. Most
authors treat the plot like a straight
line. But urban fantasy needs interwoven
mysteries, and that's where this next
trope comes in, known as mystery
threads. You have the mundane reality
and the supernatural underbelly. So, the
mystery needs to exist on both levels.
Use causality. Revelation in thread A
should trigger an escalation in thread
B, and if the hero finds a mundane clue,
it forces a magical question. If they
upset a vampire in the political thread,
it hinders the murder investigation.
Don't let subplots exist in isolation.
Every thread must matter to the
protagonist's ultimate goal. When you
get to the climax, you want all of these
threads to converge on a single point in
time. The political conspiracy solves
the murder, which reveals the hero's
power. And then the next trope is called
sanctuary. Sometimes the characters need
a break, or rather a different kind of
stress. That brings us to the sanctuary.
And this is a neutral ground. the
nightclub owned by an ancient party or
the council hall. Do not treat this as a
timeout. It's a tension magnifier. The
most intense scenes isn't always a sword
fight. It's forcing the hero to sit at a
table across from the villain because
the rules say they can't fight. The
conflict becomes psychological and
political. You need enforcement. A rule
without consequence is just a
suggestion. Establish why no one breaks
the rules. Is the owner a dragon, for
example? Is there an enchantment that
strips magic? Because when the safety of
the sanctuary is finally violated, that
is where the endgame begins. In our next
trope, if a fight does break out, maybe
the hero uses a sentient weapon. This is
the bridge between the modern and the
ancient. Think Excalibur in a pawn shop.
But don't treat it like a GPS with
attitude. It needs agency like a bad
roommate you carry in a holster. It
should cause problems. Design the
personality to foil the protagonist, and
if the hero is cautious, make the sword
bloodthirsty. Establish a motivation.
Was it forged to kill a specific
bloodline? If that goal conflicts with
the hero's current objective, you have
gold and apply costs. Does it drain the
hero's life? Does it attract predators?
Using the weapon should be a desperate
choice, not a default setting. The next
common trope is memory loss. Sometimes
to hide the truth, we use memory loss.
It gets a bad rap, but in urban fantasy,
it helps with the masquerade. If the
protagonist forgets the rules, they
become the avatar for the reader, asking
basic questions about ghouls and magical
laws. But avoid random head bumps. Tie
the amnesia to the magic system. Was it
a curse, a government wipe, a trade with
the fay? The cause determines the cure.
And don't use a magic switch to restore
everything at once. Map out a
progressive recovery with flashbacks and
false memories. A warrior who remembers
how to fight, but not why they fight has
to rediscover their morality. It turns
the recovery into character development.
Finally, we have to talk about how you
deliver all this. If you pick up 10
bestsellers in this genre, about eight
of them are probably written in
first-person past tense. This is because
of the scope. Epic fantasy needs a
bird's eye view of armies. Urban fantasy
is about one person navigating an secret
world. And so, first person is all about
voice. The narrator's personality
filters every piece of information. So,
you aren't just describing a vampire,
you're actually describing how much the
protagonist hates that vampire. And so,
this allows for the unreliable narrator.
Uh maybe the hero is wrong about how
magic works, that kind of thing. Also,
if you're writing this genre, you are
likely writing a series. But don't
confuse a cliffhanger with an unfinished
story. You must resolve the main
conflict of the current book. And if the
hero is hunting a demon, they must catch
that demon. Master the unresolved
subplot. While the battle ends, the war
escalates. Think of the Marvel postredit
scenes. The hero wins, but we see the
enemy making a phone call. This is the
stinger. It promises that safety is only
temporary. Now, I know that's a lot of
moving parts, but if you nail the
dynamics of the masquerade, the specific
type of reluctant hero, and the rules of
your magic system, you are well on your
way to writing a book that readers won't
